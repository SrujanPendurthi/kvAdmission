Retention Horizon

Every KV cache compression heuristic tested recovers at most two of eight facts at a quarter of full budget — and which two depends entirely on an accidental bias, not on anything the method measures.

Verdict

The horizon is real and severe, but the original thesis is not what explains it. Repeated compression versus one-shot compression barely matters — the difference is a single needle. What does matter: no heuristic covers the middle of the stream, and recency-like behavior emerges for free from geometric survival whether a policy intends it or not.

What was run

Eight facts of the form The secret code for <topic> is <6 digits> were planted at even intervals through an 8192-token stream of prose. The stream was prefilled in 128-token chunks; after each chunk the KV cache was compressed to a fixed budget B, per layer, with the first four tokens pinned as sinks. Each fact was then queried from the end of the stream against the compressed cache. Recall is exact-substring match on the generated code.

Five policies: full (no compression, the ceiling), recent (StreamingLLM-style window), h2o (accumulated attention), snapkv (attention voted over a 32-token observation window), and random at matched budget. Four budgets: 256, 512, 1024, 2048.

The full cache answers every question. At a quarter of that budget the best any method manages is two — and the four middle needles, d=2521 through d=5603, were never recovered by any policy at any budget.

Finding 2 — Recency is not a choice, it is a side effect

random scored identically to recent at every budget. That looked like a bug. It is a mechanism, and it turns out to be the most useful thing the spike produced.

Because compression runs every chunk, the cache never exceeds B + 128. Each round therefore discards only 128 of B+128 tokens — retention per round is B/(B+128), not B/8192. A token planted at distance d must survive d/128 independent rounds, so survival decays geometrically with distance:

P(survive) = ( B / (B + chunk) ) d / chunk

At B=2048 that is 0.941^(d/128). Against the observed random results:

The cutoff lands exactly where the model puts it. Any policy that does not actively protect distant tokens degenerates into recency, because geometric decay produces recency for free. This reframes the problem: a compression policy is not choosing what to keep so much as choosing what to rescue from an exponential that is already running.

Finding 3 — The heuristics are complementary, and both miss the middle

H2O's accumulated attention sum favors tokens that have been visible to the most query rows — that is, the oldest. Run one-shot, it recovered d=6598 and d=7657: the two oldest needles, and nothing else. recent recovered d=448 and d=1474: the two newest.

Both score 2/8. They are covering opposite ends of the same stream with opposite biases, and neither bias has anything to do with whether a token is actually useful. The middle of the context is a dead zone that no current heuristic addresses.

Why this matters more than the original hypothesis

A learned policy does not have to beat H2O at H2O's own game. It has to cover the region where every existing method scores zero — and that region is four of the eight needles, at every budget tested. The headroom is not marginal.

Finding 4 — The schedule barely matters (negative result)

The spike was designed to test whether compressing repeatedly, as a streaming setting requires, destroys information that one-shot compression preserves. If so, the episodic formulations used in the literature would be hiding a failure that only appears in a continuing setting.

One needle each way, in both directions. The episodic-versus-continuing gap is not demonstrated by this experiment. Building a paper's central claim on it would have been building on nothing — which is precisely what the spike was for.

SnapKV actually got worse one-shot, which fits: its observation window scores against the final 32 query positions, so applied once to a full 8192 cache it simply reproduces a short recency window over a much larger candidate set.

Recommendation

  • Drop "continual RL" as the framing. The schedule effect is a single needle. The name would have drawn fire from the continual-learning community anyway, and now there is no result behind it.
  • Make the middle-of-context dead zone the thesis. Four of eight needles are unrecoverable by every published heuristic at every budget tested. That is a concrete, measurable gap with a clean story: existing methods encode position biases, not utility.
  • Keep the geometric-survival result. It is a quantitative mechanism that predicts observed cutoffs, it explains why random and recency are indistinguishable, and it motivates why a policy must act against a decay process rather than simply rank tokens.
  • Keep admission control and the frozen-base RL setup. Both survive the literature review as an unoccupied intersection, and the reward now has an actual target: recall in the middle band, where the baselines are at zero.

What this does not show

A spike buys an answer, not a result. This one ran a single model (Qwen3-1.7B), a single stream, a single seed, and eight needles per cell — no error bars, and a one-needle difference is well inside noise. The H2O and SnapKV implementations are simplified reimplementations scored at token granularity across all heads, not the reference versions; their absolute numbers should not be compared to published ones. The needle task is synthetic and rewards verbatim retention, which is the easiest case for a cache and the least like real long-context work. Recall is exact substring match, so a model that has the fact but phrases the answer differently counts as a miss.

Everything in spike/ is throwaway — it exists to answer this question and should not become the training harness.
